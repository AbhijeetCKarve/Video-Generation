01  {curious} Imagine an ordinary chessboard and eight queens. | How do you place all eight so that no queen can attack another?
02  {excited} There are more than four billion ways to put eight queens on sixty-four squares. | Only ninety-two of them work!
03  {warm} Welcome to the DAA series. | I'm Abhijeet Karve, and I'll guide you through every algorithm, step by step.
04  {explain} This is Unit Three, Backtracking. | Today we solve two classic problems: the eight queens problem, and graph coloring.
05  {explain} Both are favourite exam questions, | so stay till the end for the exam tips.
06  {explain} Quick recap. | Backtracking builds the answer one choice at a time.
07  {explain} After every choice, we check a bounding function. | If the partial answer can never become a solution, we stop and go back.
08  {warm} That is why backtracking is so much faster than trying everything. | Whole branches of the search tree are cut away early.
09  {explain} First, the rules. | A queen attacks every square in her row, her column, and both diagonals.
10  {explain} Since two queens can never share a row, | we simply put queen k in row k.
11  {explain} So the whole answer is just a list x, | where x of k is the column of the queen in row k.
12  {emphatic} Now the real test. | A new queen is safe only if no earlier queen is in the same column, or on the same diagonal.
13  {explain} Two queens share a diagonal when the difference of their columns | equals the difference of their rows.
14  {explain} This check is the function Place of k and i. | It returns true only if queen k can safely go in column i.
15  {curious} Let's see it in action on a smaller board, with four queens, | and draw the state space tree as we go.
16  {warm} Queen one goes in column one. | That is the first node of our tree.
17  {explain} For queen two, columns one and two are attacked, so those branches die. | Column three is safe.
18  {emphatic} But now look at row three. | Every column is attacked. Dead end!
19  {explain} So we backtrack. | Queen two moves to column four, and queen three finds column two.
20  {emphatic} Row four is completely blocked again. | We backtrack all the way to queen one.
21  {warm} Queen one moves to column two. | Queen two goes to column four, | and queen three to column one.
22  {excited} And queen four fits in column three. | Solved! The answer is two, four, one, three.
23  {explain} Look at the tree. | The red nodes were killed by the bounding function, so we never searched below them.
24  {excited} Now watch the same algorithm on the full eight by eight board.
25  {explain} After one hundred and thirteen placements, it finds the first solution: | one, five, eight, six, three, seven, two, four.
26  {explain} If we keep going, backtracking finds all ninety-two solutions, | while checking only a tiny part of those four billion boards.
27  {explain} Here is the algorithm. | N Queens of k tries every column i for row k.
28  {explain} If Place says it is safe, we set x of k to i. | If k equals n, we print the solution, otherwise we move on to row k plus one.
29  {explain} In the worst case the tree has about n factorial leaves, | so the time complexity is order of n factorial.
30  {curious} Now our second problem. | Can you schedule five exams in just three time slots?
31  {explain} Each subject is a vertex. | An edge means some students take both subjects, so those two exams cannot share a slot.
32  {explain} This is graph coloring. | Give every vertex one of m colors, so that no two adjacent vertices get the same color.
33  {explain} The smallest such m is called the chromatic number of the graph.
34  {explain} Just like the queens, | x of k is the color of vertex k, and we fill the vertices one by one.
35  {warm} Here m is three: red, green and blue. | Vertex one gets red.
36  {explain} Vertex two touches vertex one, so red is not allowed. | It gets green.
37  {explain} Vertex three touches both one and two. | Red and green are taken, so it gets blue.
38  {warm} Vertex four only touches three and five. | Red is fine.
39  {emphatic} Now vertex five touches green, blue and red. | No color is left. Dead end!
40  {explain} So we backtrack to vertex four, | and try its next color, green.
41  {excited} Now vertex five can be red. | All five exams fit into three slots!
42  {explain} In code, Next Value of k finds the next color | that no adjacent vertex is already using.
43  {explain} There are m to the power n possible colorings, | and each check takes order n time, so the worst case is order of n times m to the n.
44  {curious} Let's try one more example, the kind you will see in exams. | Can this graph be colored with only two colors?
45  {explain} We draw the state space tree with m equal to two. | Vertex one takes color one, so vertex two must take color two.
46  {emphatic} Vertex three touches both of them, so both colors die. | We backtrack, and every other branch dies the same way.
47  {explain} The whole tree is dead, so two colors are not enough. | This graph has a triangle, and a triangle always needs three colors.
48  {excited} With m equal to three, vertex three takes color three, | and vertex four takes color two. Solved!
49  {warm} So the chromatic number of this graph is three. | In the exam, draw the tree exactly like this, with the dead nodes crossed out.
50  {explain} Notice the pattern. | Both problems use the same control abstraction: choose, check the bound, go deeper, and backtrack.
51  {emphatic} Exam tip one. | You will often be asked to draw the state space tree for four queens. Mark the killed nodes clearly.
52  {emphatic} Exam tip two. | Write the Place function, and explain the diagonal condition in words.
53  {emphatic} Exam tip three. | For graph coloring, show the color array at every step, and point out where you backtrack.
54  {warm} To recap: | backtracking builds a solution step by step, | and kills a branch the moment it breaks a rule.
