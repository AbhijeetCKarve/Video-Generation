01  Imagine you are in a maze. At every junction you pick a path, and at a dead end, you walk back to the last junction and try another.
02  That simple idea, go forward, and step back when stuck, is one of the most powerful strategies in computer science: backtracking.
03  Welcome to the DAA series. I'm Abhijeet Karve, and I'll guide you through every algorithm, step by step.
04  This is Unit Three, video one, the foundation of backtracking. Today: the state space tree, explicit and implicit constraints, depth first search, and the control abstraction.
05  Every one of these is a direct exam question, so stay till the end for the exam tips.
06  Here is our problem. Three students, Aman, Bhavna and Chirag, must sit on three seats in a row.
07  But Aman and Bhavna chat all the time, so the teacher says they must not sit next to each other.
08  Brute force would first write down every possible arrangement, and only then check each one.
09  Three choices for each seat: three times three times three, twenty seven arrangements to build and test.
10  Backtracking is smarter. It fills one seat at a time, and checks the rules after every single choice.
11  The moment a partial arrangement breaks a rule, it is thrown away, with everything that could have grown from it.
12  First, how we write a solution. Backtracking always builds the answer as a tuple: x one, x two, up to x n.
13  Here, x i is the student on seat i. So the tuple A, C, B means Aman, then Chirag, then Bhavna.
14  Every backtracking problem has two kinds of rules: explicit constraints and implicit constraints.
15  Explicit constraints restrict each x i on its own, to a set of allowed values. Here, every seat must hold A, B or C.
16  All tuples that satisfy the explicit constraints form the solution space: here, the twenty seven arrangements.
17  Implicit constraints relate the x i's to each other, and decide which tuples are real answers.
18  Here there are two. No student can sit on two seats, and Aman and Bhavna must not be neighbours.
19  Remember: explicit is about one variable, implicit is about how the variables relate.
20  Now let's organise the search as a tree, called the state space tree. The root is the empty arrangement.
21  Level one chooses seat one, level two chooses seat two, and level three chooses seat three. Each edge is one choice.
22  Seat one gets Aman. That is a new node.
23  For seat two, Aman is already seated, so that branch dies. Bhavna would sit next to Aman, so that dies too. Chirag is fine.
24  For seat three, only Bhavna is left, and she is not next to Aman. A, C, B. Our first answer!
25  Now we backtrack, all the way up to seat one, and try Bhavna there.
26  The same thing happens in mirror image. B, C, A is our second answer.
27  Finally, Chirag on seat one. Aman and Bhavna are now forced together on seats two and three, so everything below dies.
28  Done. Two answers, from only twenty four nodes, instead of the thirty nine in the full tree.
29  Some names you must know. Every node in this tree is a problem state.
30  Nodes whose path gives a complete tuple are solution states, and those that satisfy every constraint are answer states.
31  While searching, a live node is a node that has been generated, but whose children have not all been generated yet.
32  The E node is the live node being expanded right now. A dead node is never expanded again.
33  The test that kills nodes is the bounding function. It returns false when a partial tuple can never become an answer.
34  Now look at the order in which the nodes were created.
35  We always went as deep as possible first, and came back up only when we were stuck. That is depth first search.
36  Going level by level would be breadth first search, the idea behind branch and bound, in video three point four.
37  Now the general algorithm, the control abstraction. Backtrack of k fills position k, once x one to x k minus one are chosen.
38  T gives every possible value for x k. That is where the explicit constraints come in.
39  B k is the bounding function, where the implicit constraints are checked. Only values that pass it are kept.
40  If the tuple is a complete answer, we write it out. If there are more positions to fill, we call Backtrack of k plus one.
41  For our seating problem, T is the three students, and B checks that nobody is seated twice and Aman is not next to Bhavna.
42  Its speed depends on four things: the time to generate x k, how many values satisfy the explicit constraints, the time for the bounding function, and how many values pass it.
43  In the worst case the tree is still exponential, but a good bounding function makes backtracking fast in practice.
